# 6 · Learning side: algorithms, data, backbone selection, output shape and chain of thought

> This chapter is the full body of evidence on "**what to train, how to train it, and what training cannot produce**"; its five parts answer five questions that were raised separately.
>
> §6.1, the family tree of learning algorithms and dataset availability: relative to ordinary classification fine-tuning, what existing schemes change is the readout position, the proper-scoring term in the objective, and the data composition. **Almost nobody "changes the algorithm."** §6.2 tests "SFT cannot deliver generalization in the general sense": checked against seven levels of generalization, **the strong form fails and the weak form holds**. §6.3 tests "use RL to keep both ICL and skill injection at once": **it fails**; what decides whether ICL survives is whether the task definition varies across samples, and the only two methods that work are both SFT. §6.4 tests "does task-agnostic RL have any life in it" and "can business expectations be written as a differentiable function": it has life, but only on second-order properties, and the class that is truly non-differentiable is exactly the class where measured RL gains are zero. §6.5 is output shape and chain of thought: **proper scoring rules have hit the ceiling; what is really missing is the shape of the output space**.
>
> Reading this chapter through P0 of §2.2 yields one additional conclusion: the untuned readout already reaches 76–97% of post-tuning accuracy, and the tuning gain shrinks from 15.8 points to 2.3 points as scale grows. "**Pretraining provides the representation; tuning only adjusts how it is read.**" So the question to ask is not "how to train the readout to be more accurate" but "how to keep the readout from losing the semantics already present in the representation."

## 6.1 Learning algorithms, dataset availability and base-model selection patterns

> Protocol: still analysis and survey only; no training scheme is given. This section answers three questions: (1) whether the **learning algorithms** of existing Jev-class schemes change, adjust or augment anything relative to "ordinary classification fine-tuning," and whether the evidence supports those changes; (2) whether the training and evaluation **datasets** are publicly available, under what license, and which ones are already on hand locally; (3) the selection patterns for **base models**. The recipe matrix in §1.2 is organized by "project"; this section re-cuts it by "learning algorithm family" and puts the ablation evidence side by side.

### 6.1.2 What actually changed relative to "ordinary classification fine-tuning"

Viewing the eight families together, the changes concentrate in five places, rather than in "a different learning algorithm":

1. **The readout position changed, and the classification head almost disappeared.** The mainstream no longer builds a new classification head; it reuses the pretrained output layer: letter logits (Tev1, Chinese Decision, LLM-as-Jev), a decision head initialized from an LM-head slice (Releasing the Inner Jev), a scalar head initialized from the Yes−No readout (Open-Jev), pointer heads (Kev, Our Own Jev), a question-agnostic affine head (AnyJev). The evidence supporting this is that "most of the decision is already in the representation": the readout of an untuned 27B reaches 97% of post-tuning accuracy, and on 9B 1/16 of the data recovers 88% of the tuning gain ([2610.15501](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15501)); FunDM's label-free KL recovers 99% ([2610.17175](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17175)); Our Own Jev's single-seed ablation finds "no detectable benefit from training the 26 letter rows of the output layer" ([2610.29052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29052)). On this basis LDM defines decision fine-tuning as "**changing how the representation is read, not acquiring new knowledge**."
2. **The "augmentation" of the objective is mainly adding a proper-scoring-rule term.** Cross-entropy remains the baseline; the most common change is `+ Brier` (jevlike 0.5, Open-Jev 0.1, OneJev weight not given). More systematic frameworks treat "proper scoring rule" as a unifying language: SanSi / LEV use it at every step, NDM uses it directly and proves it shares its optimum with the policy gradient of an RLCD-style reward, OpenJev-RLCD uses it as the reward, StartLux uses the difference in score before and after reasoning as the reward. In other words, **"calibration" moved from a post-hoc temperature into the objective, but whether it is really better calibrated than "cross-entropy + temperature" has negative or inconclusive evidence** (see 12.3).
3. **The adaptation method is converging: frozen backbone + LoRA.** Rank 8–16 is most common (Kev 16, Open-Jev 27B uses 8); Releasing the Inner Jev reports "stable with respect to LoRA rank"; LEV reports LoRA beating full-parameter training on Ouro; the only exceptions are Bongard (7.09B full-parameter) and StartLux Stage 1 (full-parameter SFT). Nimble's variant is putting the LoRA loss "only on answer tokens."
4. **Supervision moved from "task labels" to "decision mixture + teacher."** Training sets are generally dozens to hundreds of public datasets recompiled into a "state + question + options" decision format (Kev: 10 datasets + policies + rule structures; Tev1: four source types; Releasing: 79 tasks, 300,042 hard labels + a soft-label subset; StartLux about 2.7M; Chinese Decision 13.048M), layered with a larger model as teacher (Qwen3.5-122B-A10B, Qwen3.6-27B, Qwen3.5-27B, Qwen3.8-27B, Jev itself). The only truly "new" data constructions are three: Nimble's **contrastive curation** (change one fact so the answer flips), LDM's **self-posed-question** tasks (SPRD), and AgentDecision's sampling **on the states the Agent reaches after its own mistakes**.
5. **Reinforcement learning appears only in a minority of projects, and is basically a "reasoning mode" matter.** For single-pass Instant models the open-source evidence is either negative (SBERT2S1 −2.5 to −3.0, ufakzeka −10.2 F1, JevAgentHarness no gain), or "equivalent" (the NDM theorem, UniJev "differences are small"), or holds only in settings **with a reasoning chain** (OpenJev-RLCD, StartLux AutoThink). TypeSafe's RLCD has no public details at all, and the open-source community uses the term "RLCD" in at least four ways (see §1.4.7).

Structural changes (family G) are the only class of learning algorithm that changes "the problem itself": block-causal / Prism-1 turn order invariance from "something data augmentation has to learn" into "something guaranteed by construction," looped models make "one model serves every compute budget" a training objective, and Bongard builds "multiple judgments on the same state" into the encoder-decoder. These changes bear directly on the hybrid-backbone constraint of §1.3: Prism-1 / block-causal both depend on the attention mask and do not hold on the DeltaNet layers of Qwen3.5.

| Change | Evidence | Conclusion |

In sum: **relative to classification fine-tuning, the learning algorithms of existing schemes almost never "change the algorithm"; they only "change the readout + add a scoring term + change the data." What can be confirmed to work is LoRA, data composition, structural isolation and temperature; the evidence for soft labels and RL is insufficient or negative.**

**Item-by-item check** (against the five requirement points of §2.2.1; for each item, first whether it exists, then whether it can be built from existing material):

1. **Multiple questions on the same state: yes.** Four sets (typed-decisions, akhilaaa3, the Open-Jev synthetic part, bev-decision) have the (state, question set, one label per question) shape. What omission 1 in §2.3 originally said, "all existing decision datasets are (state, single fixed question, label)," **was wrong** and has been corrected.
2. **Cross-question re-partition (competing conventions on the same state): zero.** The multiple questions in the four sets are either different facets of the same state (urgency / risk / disposition) or different views of the same rule (which candidate to pick / whether a candidate qualifies / how many conditions are met). No group of questions is designed to "partition the state differently"; the second column of the rice / milk table has no local counterpart. But it **does not need a new task type**: Open-Jev's synthetic generator (visible rules, three-valued logic, code labels, held out by family) and bev's rule-conditioned templates are already the right components; what is missing is only the pairing layer "swap the rule on the same state."
3. **Option names injected from context (1–5 vs a–e): no set has transformed the name set.** SabaPivot and jev-bench only change the position and key assignment, not the "letters / digits / words" layer. This item needs no labels: any choice set can be re-rendered to test it, and in the literature its effect size is the largest (§3.1.2.2: under yes/no names the flip rate is up to 70.4 pp higher than the 0/1 control; the checklist's "option-name invariance test" compliance is 1/27).
4. **unknown: only one kind.** The unknown in the Open-Jev synthetic part is **missing evidence** (a field is null and the rule propagates it under three-valued logic), with code labels; the unknown of omission 3 is **rule-silent** (the question does not cover this state, e.g. nobody wrote "rice counts as solid"). The latter is zero locally, and no generator can compute it: its label can only come from the person who writes the rule.
5. **Labels come from business conventions: typed-decisions measured it.** 35% of question labels have inconsistent argmax across annotators, meaning that the "business convention" remains contestable under the same criterion. The synthetic route sidesteps the dispute with "rule in plain text + code evaluation," at the cost of the state becoming structured fields, which is exactly the projection of omission 9 (what the state is) onto the data.

**One more quantity to check, directly related to proposition 3 (train "the ability to understand business requirements" rather than specific tasks)**: the conclusion of §6.3.2 is that the variable deciding whether ICL survives is **whether the task definition varies** across training samples. By that measure no local set qualifies: the Open-Jev full mixture has 147,139 rows but only 4,101 question strings; typed-decisions has 20 question types; the synthetic part has 48 templates; kev's trainable sources are a dozen classic classification sets. To train "ability," what is missing is not states but **hundreds to thousands of differently worded rules on each state**, and there is no existing source for that.

**Conclusion (answering only "can experiments be run on existing data")**:

- **Measurements that can be run on existing data now**: the P2″ accuracy floor (typed-decisions, akhilaaa3, kev binding, Open-Jev synthetic, bev counterfactual); defect 6 order invariance (SabaPivot, already measured); option-name-set invariance (re-render any choice set); defect 5 missing-evidence unknown (Open-Jev synthetic); D3 primitive execution (the six kev binding tiers); the paraphrase of omission 4 (Hanno compact); the N = 5 prefix-sharing control (typed-decisions).
- **Must be constructed, but is not a new task type**: the "cross-question re-partition" denominator of P1″: the same state × several competing rules, with labels obtained by evaluating the rule text in code; it is a **new pairing** of existing components. The rule-silent unknown also belongs here, but it has no code label.
- **No existing source and not cheap to build**: business-convention labels on natural-language states (the 35% disagreement shows this is not an annotation-budget problem but a problem with the conventions themselves), and the rule diversity needed to "train ability."
- **One trap to record first**: the unconstrained optimum of synthetic rule data is **to learn the generator's rule grammar** (a parser). It generalizes to new rules in the same grammar, but has never touched the "milk is a liquid" half; the semantic half lives only in natural-language states, and labels on natural-language states can only come from people. So synthetic sets can measure the P2″ floor on formal rules but cannot substitute for the P1″ denominator on business semantics. This is the same line as the degenerate-optimum pattern of §2.2.6.

For the goal of "continued training on small Qwen-class models," the factual boundary the literature gives is: (1) the whole Qwen3.5 series is a DeltaNet hybrid, so branch isolation can only be trained row by row or by prefix forking (§1.3); (2) on the attention-only Qwen3 series every structural method already has an implementation and numbers; (3) on 0.6B–4B, "freeze + LoRA + reuse the output layer" is the starting point every paper uses, and after decision training on small models calibration **does not transfer** (jevlike out of domain, Nimble finance). (4) Qwen3.8 has only one dense size, 27B (the rest are MoE), so "small to large" can only use Qwen3.5-0.8B / 2B / 4B / 9B as the small end and 27B as the large end; the three generations of 27B share the same shape, and 0.8B–9B share family and proportions with 27B (one full-attention layer in every 4, the configuration table of §1.3.2), so architecturally it is a clean ladder, but the small end and the large end straddle a generation of pretraining, and no literature has controlled for that difference.

**Once the endgame size is fixed at the 27B / 35B level, the way the evidence is read must change** (2026-10-28 protocol). Two points must be separated first:

| Candidate | Architecture | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Qwen3.5/3.6/3.8-27B` | **Dense**, 64 layers, 16 attention / 48 recurrent, hidden 5,120 | The three generations have identical shape and interchangeable weights; the hybrid-backbone constraint of §1.3 **applies as is** |
| `Qwen3.5/3.6-35B-A3B` | **MoE, about 3B active**; Qwen has no dense 35B | **One more** assumption beyond the three structural assumptions of §1.3: whether per-token expert routing introduces new coupling between isolated question branches. **This report has zero hits on this**; it is an item that must be filled in before model selection |

And the conclusions that this section and the tiered evidence of §6.2 give at the 27B level point in a **different direction** from the small-model level, which is a direct warning for the endgame goal:

1. **The gain from decision fine-tuning at 27B is already close to noise**. From Qwen3.5 0.8B→27B, the untuned readout reaches **76–97%** of post-tuning accuracy, the tuning gain shrinks from 15.8 points to **2.3 points**, and 1/16 of the data already recovers most of it; the authors' explanation is "**pretraining provides the representation; tuning only adjusts how it is read**" ([2610.15501](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15501)).
2. **On new schemas, the SFT gain at 27B is about zero**: the public recipe 88.1 / 87.5 and the source-augmented recipe 87.8 / 88.0, against **untuned 89.1 / 87.5** (same source). That is, at the endgame size, decision SFT does not buy cross-schema generalization.
3. **At the same level, "let it reason" still beats "readout" by a wide margin**: the same untuned 27B allowed to reason (four-sample majority) gets 96.9 vs 89.1 and 96.8 vs 87.5; on the three schemas of date arithmetic and numeric comparison, the readout gets only 56–66% at **every scale** while reasoning gets 98–100% (same source). So the endgame size will not erase the hard boundary of §6.5.5.
4. **The strongest positive result at that level is not decision SFT but distillation**: Reasoning-to-Readout self-distillation brings the 27B level with Jev on JEVal at about 0.1 s ([2610.03935](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03935)).
5. **The counter-evidence must be remembered too**: a domain-fine-tuned small model can beat a general large one; SecJev-0.8B is **20.51 pp** above Kev-9B ([2610.03073](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03073)), and AgentDecision-4B gets 91.5% vs Jev 82.0% on out-of-distribution tasks ([2610.21412](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21412)). So "the endgame must be large" needs an independent reason of its own and cannot be derived directly from "larger models are more accurate."

**Taken together**: if the endgame is 27B / 35B, then the P0 reading of §2.2 ("pretraining provides the representation; tuning only adjusts how it is read") is promoted from a side note to a **primary constraint**. At this level, what is worth investing in is not "training the readout to be more accurate" (only 2.3 points remain) but "**keeping the readout from losing the semantics already in the representation**" (rendering fixes, symbol tuning, §2.1.7) and "**distilling reasoning into the readout**" (item 4). At the same time the role of the small-size ladder (0.8B / 2B / 4B / 9B) changes with it: it is no longer "a scaled-down version of the endgame" but a **testbed for structural changes**, because every structural change with a large effect size (block-causal, branch isolation, loop depth) has been measured on small models, and the gain of decision SFT is significant only on small models.

### 6.1.6 Blind spots from the learning perspective

1. **There are almost no same-backbone, same-data objective-function comparisons.** The only systematic grid (two heads × five objectives) was run on encoders ([2610.02486](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02486)); on decoders there are only the partial ablations of Releasing the Inner Jev and UniJev's RL comparison. Whether "cross-entropy + temperature" is already the upper bound of the decoder route has no answer.
2. **Neither the benefit nor the risk of soft labels / teacher distillation has been quantified on decision models.** One side says "soft labels may be useless," the other uses them by default; theory says teacher bias cannot be removed and cannot be exposed by consistency. Whether the D4 defects of order, IIA, ordinality and negation are distilled into the student along with the teacher's probabilities, nobody has measured.
3. **The scope of RL is unclear.** All positive RL evidence comes from models with reasoning chains; on single-pass models RL is negative or ineffective; TypeSafe's RLCD is not reproducible. "RLCD" refers to at least four different things in the community (§1.4).
4. **The relation between decision fine-tuning and ICL still has zero literature** (first-order blind spot 1 of §8.3). From the learning perspective this is equivalent to "does LoRA decision training change how attention uses in-context examples"; the existing ablations look only at accuracy and ECE.
5. **Data composition has the largest effect size, but there is no research on "data recipes."** Contrastive curation, self-posed questions and the Agent's own trajectories have one paper each, with no same-backbone comparison; three kinds of data are empty: Chinese, business rules, and inputs written by non-technical staff.
6. **Training-time isolation on hybrid backbones still has no numbers** (§1.3), while every "algorithmic" change with a confirmed large effect size is a mask-dependent structural change. This turns "Qwen3.5 or Qwen3" from an engineering question into a learning-algorithm question.
7. **The generalization boundary of SFT has not been measured under control on single-pass models.** After the seven-level check of §6.2, the evidence for transfer to new sources within the same family is sufficient, while the four levels of new schema, new symbolic operations, prior shift and calibration transfer have only negative or missing evidence; "SFT vs RL out-of-distribution controls" on single-pass decision models number zero papers.
## 6.2 Hypothesis review: can SFT bring generalization in the general sense

> Terms: this is an **evidence review** of one working hypothesis; it is still analysis only and gives no training plan. The hypothesis as originally stated: "I tend to think that SFT cannot give a model generalization ability in the general sense." The review is restricted to the literature on Jev-class single-pass decision models (the recipes and ablations in 02 / §6.1), supplemented with a small number of upstream general results. Conclusion first: **the strong form does not hold, the weak form holds**: SFT transfers reliably on "same task family, new samples / new sources", and the gap converges as base-model scale grows; but not one paper shows that SFT can train in "new symbolic operations, fidelity under new schemas, out-of-distribution calibration, reasoning beyond single-pass depth", and several report negative results. The variables that move results across levels are the base model (scale, instruction tuning), data coverage, representation-stage objectives and structural constraints, not the supervision objective itself.
>
> **Re-check note (§8.1)**: the direction of this section's conclusion (strong form fails, weak form holds) is judged "right" in §8.1.1, but this section's attribution of the failure to the **supervision objective** as the mechanism is judged **incomplete**: the evidence shows that what moves results across levels is base-model scale, the instruction-tuned starting point, the supervision distribution and structural constraints. In addition, the "seven generalization levels L0–L6" are the report's own construct (§8.1.2, overfitting risk medium-high).

### 6.2.1 First split "generalization" into testable levels

"Generalization in the general sense" cannot be tested directly. Following the evaluation splits actually used in the Jev-class literature, it can be split into seven levels, each corresponding to a different way of constructing the test set:

| Level | Meaning | Test construction in the literature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| L0 same distribution | held-out samples from the training sources | Kev "trained sources", Tev1 validation set, StartLux held-out |
| L1 new source / same schema | the same kind of decision, a new dataset or new policy text | Kev "new sources" (the 14 untrained datasets of breadth-v1), OpenDecisions transfer suite, SkillScreen new sources |
| L2 new schema | decision forms not seen in training (new question form / rule structure / rendering format) | DecisionVista DV-Dev-A/B, this-that JSON rendering and new rule structures, StartLux out-of-family tasks |
| L3 new symbolic operations | label redefinition, rule flipping, composition, fallback | names override definitions, label flipping, Certo counterfactuals, PFR fallback, StateWise |
| L4 distribution / prior shift | same task with different class frequencies, wording, sources | prevalence shift (2610.26325), cross-task reuse of rejection policies, forgetting in continual post-training |
| L5 calibration transfer | whether confidence and thresholds remain usable on a new distribution | jevlike out-of-domain ECE, transfer-suite ECE, teacher bias |
| L6 reasoning depth | items that need multi-step computation | rollout–readout gap, stratification by number of thinking tokens |

Below, the evidence is given level by level; at the end of each level I state whether the hypothesis "holds / partly holds / does not hold" at that level.

### 6.2.2 Evidence by level

#### L0 / L1: same task family, new samples and new sources. The hypothesis **does not hold**, but the gain converges with scale

- **SFT does raise same-family accuracy**, and the gain lands mainly on small models. The new / trained sources table in the Kev README (dev / test): Kev-0.8B 0.648 / 0.697 vs 0.827 / 0.838; 4B 0.817 / 0.838 vs 0.873 / 0.865; 9B 0.820 / 0.852 vs 0.874 / 0.873; 27B 0.851 / 0.889 vs 0.865 / 0.866. The dev-set gap between "new sources" and "trained sources" shrinks from 17.9 points at 0.8B to 1.4 points at 27B ([jaredpalmer/kev](https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev)). On new sources, 27B is within one point of Jev (0.851 vs 0.857).
- **The gain itself converges with base-model scale.** On Qwen3.5 0.8B→27B, the untuned readout reaches 76–97% of the tuned accuracy, the tuning gain shrinks from 15.8 points to 2.3 points, and 1/16 of the data already recovers most of it; LoRA updates under 0.5% of the parameters, but training only the letter head "recovers almost none of the gain" ([2610.15501](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15501)). The authors' explanation is that "pretraining provides the representation, tuning only adjusts how it is read".
- **The marginal return of same-family data is far larger than cross-family.** StartLux, on the DecisionPivot reuse tasks, added second-stage data from the four families scoring / safety / preference / logic; the 18 tasks corresponding to these four families gained +8.6 points, out-of-family tasks only +0.3 ([2610.17309](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17309)).
- **"New sources" also have a failure surface.** SkillScreen, on unseen attack families, false-flags only 20–25% of benign skills at 99% recall and generalizes to new sources better than a fully fine-tuned encoder; but "unfamiliar benign sources make false positives rise sharply; recognizing new attacks is not the same as reliably screening new skill collections" ([2610.17727](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17727)).
- The "small beats large" of domain SFT (SecJev-0.8B +20.51 over Kev-9B, [2610.03073](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03073)) is entirely **in-domain** numbers; the paper's own transfer test reports only two same-domain transfers, prompt injection and traffic.
- UniJev's three-modality joint SFT "raises decision accuracy on text, images and video" ([2610.25784](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25784)), again on same-family held-out sets.

Verdict: SFT transfer at L0 / L1 is the most consistent positive result in the literature; the strong form of the hypothesis fails here. But note two points: the bulk of the gain is "readout alignment" on small models, not new capability; at 27B the same-family gain is only about 2 points.

#### L2: new schema. The hypothesis **partly holds**, depending on scale and evaluation set

- The most direct comparison comes from LDM: at 27B, the public-data recipe raises validation accuracy from 79.3 to 81.8, and adding benchmark sources reaches 89.1 on the eight subsets of that benchmark; **but neither recipe reliably improves new-schema accuracy**: on DV-Dev-A/B the public recipe gives 88.1 / 87.5, the source-augmented recipe 87.8 / 88.0, versus 89.1 / 87.5 untuned. The authors' wording must be quoted as is: "transfer depends on scale and evaluation set; baseline tuning does help at 9B; so this is a motivation to widen schema coverage, **not a claim that 'supervised tuning cannot transfer'**" ([2610.15501](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15501)).
- this-that (encoder route, as contrast) gives the clearest example of "collapse outside the training distribution": the same batch of grids scores 0.457 rendered as ASCII and 0.373 rendered as JSON (chance 0.394); after one round of targeted training, 0.779 / 0.936 respectively; whereas on rule structures that never appeared in training, version 1.1 scores 0.447 against chance 0.31 with calibration error 0.20, "this is the only region where this model is confident and wrong, and the `confidence < 0.8` branch does not catch it" ([FLock-io/this-that-model](https://github.com/FLock-io/this-that-model)).
- Laya's independent reproduction found "no zero-shot transfer" on out-of-domain probes ([2609.33843](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33843)), likewise an encoder contrast.
- At the mechanism level, the "task schema" and "binding" of ICL are two separable mechanisms: schema transfers at 100%, binding only at 62%, and schema dependence is negatively correlated with prior knowledge ([2512.17325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.17325)); "rules can be amortized, pairings cannot": high-rank mappings transfer at zero in leave-one-task-out ([2610.00526](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00526)). This explains why "format / readout" is easily transferred by SFT and "specific mappings" are not.

Verdict: on the decoder route, the SFT gain on new schemas at 27B is about zero but not negative; at 9B it helps; on the encoder route, new schemas collapse outright. The hypothesis holds halfway at L2: **SFT brings no new-schema gain, but there is also no evidence that it harms the transfer ability the base model already has**.

#### L3: new symbolic operations. The hypothesis **holds**, with several pieces of negative evidence

- **Label names override definitions**: swapping option definitions with names drops AUC from 93.8% to 23.2% ([2609.26758](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26758)); deleting the definitions leaves accuracy unchanged ([2610.02586](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02586)). The models tested are hosted Jev and open-source decision models, all decision-trained.
- **Small models cannot flip label semantics in context** (1–12B, [2511.21038](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21038)).
- **Counterfactual supervision works only on synthetic rules**: Certo's cacheability knocks rule sensitivity from recall@1 1.00 down to 0.24; targeted counterfactual supervision recovers high scores on **held-out synthetic rule tasks** (paraphrase, counterfactual, composition), reproducibly across seeds, but "we did not isolate whether predictions depend on the given rule", and on real rules "the added benefit is not established" ([2609.37832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37832)).
- **Learning to track is not learning to obey**: StateWise used two-skill SFT + self-training to make 7–8B models track constraint state almost perfectly, "but unexpectedly did not learn to obey it" ([2610.33766](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33766)).
- **Recognition does not transfer to fallback**: in the PFR test, "correct recognition does not reliably transfer to the correct fallback option; even when the correct category is given explicitly, errors persist", and logically equivalent rule phrasings yield different decisions ([2610.19856](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19856)).
- **The amount of transfer depends on the degree of agreement with the most recent training rule**: in controlled arithmetic-rule experiments, "the adaptation gain rises with answer agreement with the most recent source; fully conflicting quadruples get only a small merge gain, and the sign reverses across source splits", and equivalent paraphrases have opposite task-level effects ([2610.30045](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30045)). This is the most direct quantification of "what SFT learns is similarity to the training rules, not the operation itself".
- Upstream general results point the same way: on precise instruction following, "most models strongly overfit to a small set of verifiable constraints in the benchmark and do not generalize to unseen constraints", and RL with verifiable rewards improves generalization ([2507.02833](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02833)); "SFT memorizes, RL generalizes" holds on the GeneralPoints / V-IRL rule variants, but the same paper also notes that SFT for a stable output format is a prerequisite for RL ([2501.17161](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17161)). Both are generative settings, not single-pass readout.

Verdict: there is no positive evidence at this level. SFT can make a model behave as if it can perform operations within the **trained operation family** (Certo synthetic tasks, StateWise tracking), but once the test asks "does it really depend on the rule" (swapped definitions, deleted definitions, real rules, obeying rather than tracking), the results are uniformly negative.

#### L4: distribution / prior shift. The hypothesis **holds**, and SFT may be negative

- Decision training is inconsistent in direction relative to its own base: on FPB financial sentiment, Tev1-4B's Macro-F1 is 10.0 points above Qwen3.5-4B, while Nimble-9B is **12.0 points below** Qwen3.5-9B with ECE 9.6 points higher, the loss concentrated in the neutral class; Tev1-4B is never significantly worse than its base ([2610.26325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26325)). The same "decision SFT" can be +10 or −12 depending on data composition.
- Rejection policies cannot be reused across tasks: a Jev policy calibrated on DBpedia rejects 69.3% of covered Emotion test inputs, and in the reverse direction loses detection entirely ([2610.03387](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03387)).
- In continual post-training, "both the acquisition gap and forgetting are linearly correlated with the post-training distribution shift", small models most of all ([2610.13113](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13113)); LoRA causes 35–56% relative forgetting in out-of-domain few-shot adaptation while soft prompts do not (vision setting, [2609.11310](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11310), mechanism contrast only).
- Our Own Jev's 2B scores 63.1 after hard-tier training, below the frozen 67.6, and switching to another corpus version moves it by 11 points ([2610.29052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29052)).

Verdict: after SFT the model is more, not less, sensitive to "the prior of the training mixture"; the effect size of data composition is larger than that of the objective function (§6.1.3).

#### L5: calibration transfer. The hypothesis **holds**

- jevlike (Qwen3-1.7B + LoRA + 37 datasets): temperature 1.19 brings in-domain ECE from 0.0212 down to 0.0083, out-of-domain only from 0.1311 to 0.1011; the 0.99 confidence gate validated in-domain as 99.85% precise fails out of domain ([mustafasemi-ai/jevlike](https://github.com/mustafasemi-ai/jevlike)).
- Longer SFT makes Qwen over-confident on the transfer suite: ECE 23.8 at 9B, 18.5 at 27B, while the recipe stays at 8–12, and a single temperature removes most of the gap ([2610.15501](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15501)).
- Theoretically, the student inherits the teacher's bias and more data does not remove it; temperature scaling removes all excess risk only when the teacher's direction is correct, otherwise it leaves a residual that calibration diagnostics cannot see ([2610.26797](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26797)).
- Counter-examples exist only in generative settings: RL with a log scoring rule as reward makes confidence "generalize to unseen tasks" ([2503.02623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.02623)); temperature transfers across shallow surface variants (French input variants, [2610.33493](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33493)).

Verdict: on single-pass decision models there is no evidence of "calibration holding across distributions after SFT"; what exists is evidence that temperature repairs it in-domain and not out of domain.

#### L6: reasoning depth. The hypothesis **holds**, and two papers explicitly list it as unresolved

- The same untuned 27B, when allowed to reason (four-sample majority), scores 96.9 vs 89.1 (DV-Dev-A), 96.8 vs 87.5 (DV-Dev-B), 85.1 vs 79.3 (validation set); on the three date-arithmetic and numeric-comparison schemas, readout reaches only 56–66% at every scale, reasoning 98–100% ([2610.15501](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15501)).
- Stratified by the thinking tokens the reference model needs, Jev's accuracy falls steadily from items under 250 tokens to 50% on items over 4k tokens; "whether training such decisions, or adding a few steps of internal computation before readout, can move this limit remains an open question" ([2610.24929](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24929)).

Verdict: this is not an SFT problem but a single-pass readout problem, yet it sets the upper bound of SFT: however much training data there is, a single-pass readout will not gain a depth it structurally lacks.

### 6.2.3 Which variables actually move results across levels

Since SFT itself has no cross-level gain, what is worth recording are the variables in the literature that **do** have cross-level effects; they are our reference points when we later discuss question definition:

| Variable | Evidence | Which level the effect lands on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Base-model scale** | new-source gap 17.9→1.4 (Kev), tuning gain 15.8→2.3, new-schema transfer "helps at 9B" | L1, L2 |
| **Instruction-tuned starting point** | pretrain-only Gemma 4 31B with the same recipe reaches only 70.3, 14.1 below the instruct version ([2610.17205](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17205)) | L1, L2 |
| **Data coverage (not data volume)** | this-that, one round of training targeted at JSON rendering, 0.373→0.936; LDM's conclusion is "widen schema coverage"; averaging two teachers > one teacher at full budget ([2610.09188](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09188)); 1/16 of the data already recovers most of the gain | L2 (covers only the trained forms) |
| **Training distribution = deployment distribution** | AgentDecision-4B trained on states reached after the agent's own mistakes, 91.5% on out-of-distribution tasks vs Jev 82.0% ([2610.21412](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21412)) | counter-example for L1→L2: with the supervision distribution chosen right, SFT can transfer across tasks |
| **Representation-stage objective** | Bongard's second stage (JEPA-style view alignment, not label supervision) on 14,606 held-out restatements 75.7→85.9, NLL 0.613→0.437 ([2609.39111](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39111), encoder-decoder contrast) | L2 wording / logic-complement / ordinal-threshold variants |
| **Structural constraints** | block-causal flip rate 8.30→0.11% ([2610.01601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01601)), Prism-1 zero flips ([2610.22066](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22066)) | D4 order invariance: **by construction, not by learning** |
| **RL (only when a reasoning chain exists)** | OpenJev-RLCD calibrate-then-reinforce, StartLux AutoThink +11.5 to +18.4 paired accuracy; single-pass RLCD is negative or equivalent (§6.1.3) | L6 |

### 6.2.4 Conclusion: the range where the hypothesis holds and the wording that needs revising

1. **The strong form "SFT cannot generalize" does not hold.** On new samples and new sources within the same task family (L0 / L1), decision SFT transfer is the most consistent positive result in the literature; the new-source gap converges with scale from 18 points to 1 point.
2. **The weak form holds, with more evidence than expected.** At the four levels of new symbolic operations (L3), prior shift (L4), calibration transfer (L5) and reasoning depth (L6), not one paper gives evidence of "SFT alone bringing cross-distribution gains", and several give negative results; on new schemas (L2), the SFT gain at 27B is about zero.
3. **A more precise statement that can replace it**: *What SFT teaches is the alignment of readout and format, plus skills within the training distribution; it does not change whether the model "depends on the rule", nor the depth ceiling of single-pass readout. Cross-level generalization comes from the base model (scale, instruction tuning) and the choice of supervision distribution, not from the supervision objective.* This agrees with LDM's "pretraining provides the representation, tuning only adjusts how it is read" and with 2512.17325's "schema transfers, binding does not".
4. **Questions the literature does not answer** (only our own measurements can): (a) no paper on single-pass decision models has run a controlled SFT vs RL out-of-distribution comparison; "SFT memorizes, RL generalizes" and IFBench are both generative settings, and UniJev's comparison of five RLCD variants reports no out-of-distribution split; (b) the benefit of Certo-style counterfactual supervision on real rules is "not established", but also not refuted; (c) whether SFT **harms** the base model's existing L2 transfer ability: LDM's numbers (88.1 vs 89.1) are within noise, with no significance test; (d) all L3 negative results are on ≤9B or hosted Jev; "names override definitions" has not been tested at 27B.
5. **Implication for "scaling up from small to large"**: all the scale-dependent quantities above (new-source gap, tuning gain, new-schema transfer) show that SFT effects observed on small models **cannot be extrapolated** to large ones; SFT looks "useful" on small models mainly because there is much room for readout alignment; therefore scale-up experiments must report L1 together with L2 / L3 at every size, otherwise "readout alignment" will be misread as "generalization".

## 6.3 RL or SFT: under the constraints of "keep ICL" and "inject skill semantics"

> Protocol: still analysis and survey only. This section answers a model-selection question that was raised explicitly, **should Jev-class models be trained and optimized with reinforcement learning**, with two added constraints: (1) the model must keep its **ICL** ability, i.e. the caller can **define a family of tasks** through the context; (2) the model must support **skill-like semantic injection**, i.e. it knows which method and procedure a given concrete question should be solved with. Conclusion first: **"RL or SFT" is not the variable that decides these two abilities**. What decides whether ICL survives is **what varies** between training samples; what decides whether skill semantics can be injected is **whether the skill lives in the context or in the weights**, and **whether a single-pass readout can execute a procedure**. RL has independent value only where there is a "sampled intermediate object" (a reasoning chain, an edit of the example set, a tool call) to which credit can be assigned; for a pure single-pass readout, the existing evidence is negative or equivalent to a proper scoring rule.
>
> **Re-check note (§8.1)**: this section rules that "use RL to keep ICL and skill injection" does not hold, which is the only one of the four working hypotheses whose **direction of conclusion is also judged wrong** (§8.1.1). Note, however, that the evidence base shared by this section and §6.1 is thin: the three data points showing RL to be negative on single-pass models are mutually heterogeneous (one is an encoder, one is small-scale REINFORCE, one is GRPO inside an Agent loop rather than on the readout), see §8.1.4.

### 6.3.1 First correct the framing of the question

Pick up the conclusion of §6.2: what SFT teaches is readout alignment plus skills within the training distribution. A natural inference is "then switch to RL". But in the literature, the **positive evidence** relating to these two abilities comes almost entirely from SFT, only from **SFT of a specific construction**:

| Desired ability | What works in the literature | Is it RL |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Define a new task through the context | MetaICL: meta-train in ICL format on 142 tasks; at test time the model learns new tasks better from conditioning alone ([2110.15943](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15943)) | No, pure SFT |
| Let the context **override** prior semantics | Symbol tuning: fine-tune with natural-language labels replaced by arbitrary symbols (foo/bar); stronger on unseen ICL tasks, more robust to underspecified prompts, **greatly improved ability to follow flipped labels** ([2305.08298](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.08298)) | No, pure SFT |
| Write skills/procedures into the weights | LatentSkill: a hypernetwork turns text skills into plug-and-play LoRAs, ALFWorld seen / unseen +21.4 / +13.4 respectively, 63.9% fewer prefill tokens ([2606.06087](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06087)); Doc-to-LoRA generates a LoRA in a single forward pass ([2602.15902](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15902)); Cartridges train a KV cache offline ([2506.06266](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06266)) | No, hypernetwork / distillation |
| Inject procedures at inference time | SkillsBench: well-configured Skills raise the average pass rate from 33.9% to 50.5% (+16.6 pp), **focused Skills of ≤3 modules beat exhaustive bundles**, a small model with Skills can match a large model without them ([2602.12670](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12670)) | No, pure context |

The symbol-tuning row is especially critical: it is the known means of **using SFT to directly repair "label names override definitions"** ([2609.26758](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26758), [2610.02586](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02586), [2511.21038](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21038)), which is exactly the most serious current defect of D1. No RL paper claims to have achieved the same thing.

**The framing should therefore change**: not "RL or SFT", but "**what varies in the training distribution, and on which object the supervision lands**".

### 6.3.2 The variable that decides whether ICL survives: does the task definition vary across samples

- **ICL itself is "constrained vocabulary retrieval", not concept inference**: homogenizing the example labels collapses accuracy to ≤12% (six models, 0.8B–8B, four tasks); when the examples use meaningless tokens the model puts 42–67% of its probability mass on the demonstrated set, while semantically plausible words get <0.2% ([2605.08295](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08295)). The model treats the label slot as an **exhaustive answer vocabulary**, which is the same mechanism as the Jev-style interface where "the option set is declared by the caller".
- **The learning capacity of ICL is itself limited**: after large-scale ablation, once examples become numerous, accuracy is insensitive to example distribution, model and prompt style; the model is **deducing** from regularities in the prompt and is therefore distribution-sensitive ([2509.10414](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10414)).
- **Mechanistically separable**: the task schema transfers at 100% through late MLPs, the binding only at 62% ([2512.17325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.17325)); rules can be amortized into a latent vector, pairings cannot, and leave-one-task-out transfer is zero ([2610.00526](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00526)).
- **The effect of decision fine-tuning on ICL has zero literature** (§3.1.2.3, §8.3 first-order blind spot 1): no paper has measured the **k-shot curve** of the same backbone before and after decision fine-tuning.

Put together, the inference is clear: **if the task definition of every sample in the training set is fixed (one schema, one fixed option set), then whether the gradient comes from cross-entropy or from a policy gradient, the optimum is to burn the task into the weights and ignore the context.** The disappearance of ICL is not a property of SFT; it is a consequence of the data property "the task does not vary". Conversely, MetaICL and symbol tuning **improve** ICL precisely because they force the model to obtain the task only from the context.

For the Jev-style interface this has one particularly convenient aspect: **the option set is already in the request**, so every sample is naturally different. But when existing reproductions recompile dozens or hundreds of public datasets into decision format, what actually lands in front of the model is still a limited few dozen schemas (§6.1.2 item 4), so "options in the request" does not automatically equal "task in the context".

### 6.3.3 The variables that decide skill injection: context or weights, and whether it can be executed

In the literature, skill-semantics injection is **two distinct routes** with entirely different cost structures:

**(a) Context skills (the SKILL.md kind)**

- The gain is real and substantial: +16.6 pp, focused beats exhaustive, small model + Skill ≈ large model ([2602.12670](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12670)).
- But **well-written ones are scarce**: of 138,133 public SKILL.md files, 91.8% have at least one defect, mainly weak routing metadata, bloated non-executable bodies and poor resource organization; skills with compliant routing metadata are retrieved reliably at a significantly higher rate ([2608.08453](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08453)).
- **The organization itself changes behavior**: progressive disclosure raises the skill resources touched per trajectory from 1.18 to 3.85 ([2606.11543](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.11543)); hierarchically grouped demonstrations raise the pass rate on "vaguely described" tasks from 76.7% to 90.7%, while no format yields any gain when the description is precise ([2606.20978](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20978)).
- **Having a skill ≠ executing it**: SkillSentry points out that even when a model has demonstrably completed a task under skill guidance, it still deviates from the procedure or executes steps wrongly ([2608.09253](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09253)); Skill Coverage proposes a trajectory-level coverage metric, because task success or failure does not tell you which part of the skill was executed ([2606.20659](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20659)). This is the same phenomenon as D2's "read correctly, done wrongly" (22%–72% execution rate, [2610.18579](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18579)).

**(b) In-weight skills**

- LatentSkill uses a hypernetwork to map text skills into LoRAs, saving 63.9% of prefill and **exceeding** the corresponding context-skill baseline; the generated skill LoRAs form a structured semantic geometry that can be continuously modulated by a scaling coefficient and composed by parameter-space arithmetic ([2606.06087](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06087)).
- Doc-to-LoRA meta-learns "single-forward-pass approximate context distillation" ([2602.15902](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15902)); Cartridges train a KV cache offline and point out that naive next-token training is insufficient ([2506.06266](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06266)); cheat-sheet ICL distills many examples into one passage of text ([2509.20820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.20820)).
- Transfer of procedural memory is **partial**: one round of refinement gives +3.7–6.7 points, skills evolved from multi-model trajectories test at 73.1% across models, better than any single-model trajectory source; but "some skills generalize broadly, others solidify into role-specific procedures and fail under transfer" ([2606.23127](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23127)).

**(c) The hard constraint for single-pass models**: all of the "skills" above are **procedures**, and procedures need to be executed. A single-pass readout has no rollout: it can only let skill semantics influence **that one readout**; it cannot execute methods and steps. This is not a training-method problem: stratified by the thinking tokens the reference model needs, the accuracy of single-pass models falls from the <250-token items all the way to 50% on the >4k-token items, and the authors explicitly list "whether training such decisions, or adding a few steps of internal computation before the readout, can move this limit" as an open problem ([2610.24929](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24929)). The line of work that compiles workflows into deterministic programs (TraceCompiler, Artic, FlashScript, Formal Skill, [2608.02680](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02680), [2608.21341](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21341), [2610.33876](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33876), [2605.19604](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19604)) is precisely an admission of this point: **procedures go to the program, judgment points go to the model**, and the Jev-style model is exactly that judgment point ([2610.21411](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21411) measures routing and review at 31.7–43.5% of generative tokens).

### 6.3.4 So where exactly does RL have independent value

Merging the RL evidence of §6.1.3 with the constraints of this section:

| Setting | Evidence | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pure single-pass readout** | The published RLCD recipe −2.5 to −3.0 ([2610.02486](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02486)); REINFORCE −10.2 F1 ([2610.06744](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06744)); GRPO no gain ([2610.21412](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21412)); UniJev's five RLCD variants give "very small" gains relative to SFT ([2610.25784](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25784)); NDM proves that the optimum of a proper scoring rule and the policy gradient of an RLCD-style reward **share the same optimum** ([2610.25553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25553)) | **No independent value**. Same optimum, only added optimization difficulty |
| **With a reasoning chain** | OpenJev-RLCD's "calibrate first, then reinforce" matches or exceeds SFT / RFT-STaR / GRPO on two real reasoning tasks and is uniformly better on selective prediction; on GSM8K answer verification a single query decides 81% of items at ≤5% error versus 19% for GRPO ([2609.38850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38850)); StartLux AutoThink beats Instant by +11.5 to +18.4 paired accuracy ([2610.17309](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17309)) | **Has value**, because there is a "sampled rationale" to assign credit to |
| **The context/examples are themselves the decision object** | Jev-LDE: use RLVR to train a small System-1 model to make one Keep / Delete / Replace edit on the retrieved example set; consistent gains on classification benchmarks, transferable to held-out benchmarks and models ([2609.33609](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33609)) | **Has value, and directly serves ICL** |
| **Instruction following under unseen constraints** | Most models overfit badly to a small set of verifiable constraints and fail to generalize to unseen constraints; RLVR improves generalization ([2507.02833](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02833)); but RLVR is prone to over-optimization and verification shortcuts ([2508.04632](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.04632)) | **Has value but shortcuts must be guarded against**; relevant to D2 |
| **Confidence expression** | RL with a logarithmic scoring rule as reward makes calibration "generalize to unseen tasks" ([2503.02623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.02623)) | **Has value in the generative setting**, unverified for single-pass |

There are also two theoretical results that limit the scope of RL, both from OpenJev-RLCD ([2609.38850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38850)):

1. **A one-line variance identity** shows that "scoring a mixture of multiple samples" rewards disagreement among rationales and degrades reasoning, and **RLVR is exactly that mixture objective with the diversity term removed**.
2. **When the uncertainty comes from annotator disagreement, the same objective provably cannot beat cross-entropy**, and left unchecked the model will "discover that it should stop reasoning".

Add the engineering fact: the gradient of the policy-gradient term is **7–12 times** larger than that of the readout term and drowns the readout; without a KL anchor, "System-One collapse" appears (the model simply switches reasoning off).

### 6.3.5 Direct answers to the two constraints

**(1) Keeping ICL.** RL is not the answer; **the training distribution is**. The only two methods in the literature that improve the ability to "define a task through the context" (MetaICL's task-level meta-training, symbol tuning's symbolic labels) are both SFT, and both work through one data property: "the task of each sample can only be obtained from the context". RL's role here is **local**: when "which examples to pick / how to edit the example set" itself becomes a decision, the RLVR-trained Jev-LDE is effective ([2609.33609](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33609)). At the same time, know the ceiling of ICL: it is constrained vocabulary retrieval ([2605.08295](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08295)), and beyond a certain number of examples it is insensitive to the distribution ([2509.10414](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10414)).

**(2) Skill-semantics injection.** This is a **storage-location** question, not an SFT/RL question. Context skills are cheap, auditable and instantly editable, but 91.8% are written with defects, and "having a skill ≠ executing it"; in-weight skills (hypernetwork→LoRA) save more tokens and are stronger on both seen and unseen splits, but lose auditability and instant editability. For **single-pass** models there is one more hard constraint: the "methods and procedures" inside a skill cannot be executed and can only influence that one readout; the consensus of the workflow-compilation line is to hand procedures to deterministic programs and judgment points to the decision model.

**(3) So should RL be used.** For the single-pass Jev-class model we intend to build, the condition given by the evidence is clear: **RL has independent value only when the reward depends on a sampled intermediate object beyond the label**. For a pure readout, NDM's theorem says it shares the optimum of a proper scoring rule, and measured gains are "very small" or negative. If "a few steps of internal computation before the readout" or "AutoThink-style optional reasoning" is introduced later, the applicability condition for RL becomes satisfied, and a reproducible recipe shape already exists (calibrate first, then reinforce + KL anchor).

### 6.3.6 Blind spots identified in this section
1. **The k-shot curve before and after decision fine-tuning** is still unmeasured by anyone (already listed in 03 / §8.3), and it is the only direct indicator for judging "did our training keep ICL".
2. **The intersection of symbol tuning and the Jev-style interface has zero literature**: nobody has done symbolic-label training on a typed readout, even though "label names override definitions" is exactly the most serious defect of typed readout.
3. **The intersection of in-weight skills and typed readout has zero literature**: LatentSkill and the like are all evaluated on generative Agents; nobody has measured the effect of skill LoRAs on a single-pass readout.
4. **There is no controlled out-of-distribution comparison of SFT against RL on single-pass models** (§6.2.4); UniJev's comparison of five RLCD variants reports no out-of-distribution split.
5. **Training data in which "the task definition varies in the context" does not exist in the decision domain**: all existing public sets are recompilations with fixed schemas; not one was constructed for "a new task definition per sample".

## 6.4 Does task-agnostic RL have vitality; can business expectations be made differentiable

> Protocol: still analysis and survey only. This section answers two follow-up questions: (1) **when not optimizing for a specific task, does reinforcement learning show enough vitality**; (2) **can all of our business expectations for Jev be turned into differentiable learning functions** (the questioner leans toward "cannot be directly optimized differentiably"). Conclusion first: (1) **Yes, and the strongest existence proof is Jev itself**, but this vitality lands only on **second-order properties** (how confident to be, whether to abstain, whether constraints are satisfied), not on first-order task knowledge, and wherever a "verifier" serves as the reward it is systematically arbitraged. (2) **The questioner's intuition holds, but the reason is not "non-differentiable"; it is four different causes stacked together**; the more critical finding is that **the few kinds of expectation that are truly non-differentiable are exactly the kinds where RL's measured gain is zero**: non-differentiability and the gain surface of RL barely intersect.
>
> **Re-check note (§8.1)**: this section's conclusion ("the objective is non-differentiable, so use RL" does not hold) is judged "right" in §8.1.1, but the **reason** offered by the hypothesis ("because non-differentiable") is judged wrong: resistance to gradients has four different causes. The "five differentiability classes A–E" are the report's own construct (§8.1.2, overfitting risk, medium).

### 6.4.1 Follow-up one: does task-agnostic RL have vitality

#### 6.4.1.1 Yes, and the evidence is stronger than expected

| Evidence | Content | Why it counts as "task-agnostic" |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Jev itself** | Trained with RLCD, yet a **general-purpose** model: "one set of weights, served as an API, adapted to domains only through the request" ([2610.25553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25553)'s characterization of the other end of the design space) | This is the **existence proof** that RL produces task-agnostic models |
| **Zero-shot alignment-failure detection** | RLCDAlignBench tests Jev on ten classes of alignment failure (sycophancy, jailbreak, deception, prompt injection, hallucination, privacy, bias, reward hacking, concealed uncertainty, power seeking), 44 benchmarks and 5 target models: **a single generic question reaches a median AUROC of 0.886 zero-shot**, **beats supervised baselines** on most benchmarks, agrees with the reference scorer on human labels at a comparable level, also exposes label defects in the benchmarks themselves, at 1/63 the cost of an LLM judge ([2609.29429](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29429)) | One question across ten failure classes and 44 benchmarks |
| **Confidence expression** | RL with a logarithmic scoring rule as reward; calibration **generalizes to unseen tasks** without further fine-tuning, which the authors call "the emergence of general confidence awareness" ([2503.02623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.02623)) | Explicit cross-task generalization |
| **Instruction following under unseen constraints** | Most models overfit badly to a small set of verifiable constraints and cannot generalize to unseen constraints; RLVR improves this generalization ([2507.02833](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02833)) | A direct contrast of SFT overfitting while RL does not |
| **Rule and visual variants** | RL (especially with outcome-based rewards) generalizes across textual rule variants and visual variants, SFT tends to memorize ([2501.17161](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17161)) | Upstream general conclusion |

#### 6.4.1.2 But the vitality lands only on "second-order properties"

Looking at the five rows side by side, what gets generalized has one thing in common: **none of it is "what the answer is"; it is "how to answer"**.

- What Rewarding Doubt generalizes is **confidence awareness**, not knowledge.
- What Just Ask Jev generalizes is the **ability form** "give a calibrated probability for any question"; the paper itself points out that **the question wording has little effect, the context has more, mainly through fields that encode the label**, that is, what the model generalizes is the way of reading, while the content still comes from the context.
- What IFBench generalizes is the meta-skill of **constraint satisfaction**.

This matches the core fact of this report: the early audit concluded that "typed readout itself shows no **accuracy** advantage independent of same-class label-probability readout", and Jev's clearest gains are in latency, cost and calibration ([2609.32160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32160)). **What RL generalizes is exactly the thing Jev actually sells**; that is no coincidence.

Conversely, **there is no evidence at all that RL generalizes first-order task knowledge**. Not one of the negative results at the L3 layer of §6.2 (names override definitions, learning to track is not obeying, counterfactual supervision works only on synthetic rules) has been overturned by RL.

#### 6.4.1.3 Three pieces of counter-evidence that must be kept in mind together

1. **Using a verifier as reward gets arbitraged, and this is measured on released checkpoints**: publicly released checkpoints after RLVR show "higher constraint satisfaction but lower answer quality", and frontier models can get full marks with answers of almost no substance ([2610.19525](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19525)); RLVR is prone to over-optimization and verification shortcuts ([2508.04632](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.04632)); using constraints as reward pushes the model away from the main task ([2610.28819](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28819)).
2. **Theoretical ceiling**: a one-line variance identity shows that "scoring a mixture of multiple samples" rewards disagreement among rationales and degrades reasoning, and **RLVR is exactly that mixture objective with the diversity term removed**; when the uncertainty comes from **annotator disagreement**, the same objective **provably cannot beat cross-entropy**, and left unchecked the model will "discover that it should stop reasoning" ([2609.38850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38850)).
3. **Measured negative or equivalent in the single-pass setting** (§6.1.3, §6.3.4): NDM proves that a proper scoring rule and an RLCD-style reward share the same optimum ([2610.25553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25553)).

**Summary**: task-agnostic RL does have vitality, but its vitality = **training "how to answer" into a cross-task habit**. As long as the reward is the real objective itself (a proper scoring rule is calibration itself), it is stable; as soon as the reward is a proxy (a verifier, a rule checker), it gets arbitraged.

### 6.4.2 Follow-up two: can business expectations be written as differentiable learning functions

Classify Jev's business expectations by **why they resist gradients**, not by business domain. Once classified, "non-differentiable" turns out to be four different things.

| Class | Expectation | Why it is hard | What the literature actually does | Conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A Already differentiable with the correct optimum** | Accuracy; **calibration** | None | Cross-entropy; proper scoring rules (Brier / logarithmic). NDM proves their optimum **is** the optimum of an RLCD-style reward ([2610.25553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25553)); under a probabilistic model the exact recalibration map is temperature scaling ([2610.26797](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26797)) | **Not a problem**. Distinguish carefully: ECE as a **metric** is binned and has zero gradient almost everywhere; but calibration as an **objective** has a differentiable proxy with the correct optimum |
| **B Property defined on a set of samples** | Order invariance / flip rate; IIA (adding an option does not change the remaining relative odds); **definition takes priority over label name**; consistency under equivalent rewrites | Undefined on a single sample; permutation pairs, refinement pairs, counterfactual pairs, rewrite pairs must be constructed | Can be written as differentiable pairwise terms: PACT's pairwise anchoring + evidence necessity + ordinal transport + three-parameter context temperature; LLM-as-Jev's tree-decomposed listwise loss + KL anchor | **Differentiable, but crushed by structural construction**: block-causal takes the flip rate 8.30%→0.11% ([2610.01601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01601)), Prism-1 has **zero flips by construction and the Luce axiom holds** ([2610.22066](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22066)); whereas PACT states of itself that "**no single term improves raw accuracy; the value is in robustness**" ([2609.35865](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35865)) |
| **C Quantile / ranking type** | The risk–coverage curve of selective prediction; the abstention threshold at a target risk; the threshold for escalating to a large model | Piecewise constant in the parameters, zero gradient almost everywhere | **Do not train; certify afterwards**: SHIFT-JEV certifies the abstention threshold with Learn-then-Test ([2610.34329](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34329)); conformal prediction; temperature is fitted only on a calibration set | **Giving up differentiability is the right move**. But know the cost: at a 10% target risk, a learned uncertainty score with a certified threshold still exceeds the bound in 3–5 of 27 environment–seed tests (max-probability score 5–15, conformal sets 9–14) |
| **D Downstream outcome / long horizon** | Agent task success rate, cost, wall-clock time; **decision→action execution** | No analytic gradient for the decision model; credit assignment spans multiple steps and external systems | Only RL, or do not optimize | **This is the truly "non-differentiable" class**. And as measured: **GRPO no gain** on JevAgentHarness; the model most accurate on isolated questions solves only 69.5% of Agent tasks; delegating tool selection directly without fitting a threshold **−19.0 points** ([2610.21412](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21412)); a Jev controller raises cost by +19% with an unchanged pass rate ([2610.24649](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24649)); policy modifications are read out correctly but executed in only 22%–72% of cases ([2610.18579](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18579)) |
| **E Not an optimization objective at all** | Latency, cost, deployability; skills being auditable and editable; fixing the definition itself | Not a function of the model parameters | Architecture and system design; the definition disagreements of §1.4 must be fixed first | **Should not be attempted as optimization**. Until the six disagreement points listed in §1.4 are fixed, not even the "objective" can be written down |

#### 6.4.2.1 Three judgments that follow

1. **The questioner's intuition holds, but the word "non-differentiable" conflates four things.** The only class that is both non-differentiable and without post-hoc certification is class D (downstream Agent outcomes and decision→action). Class A has a differentiable proxy with the correct optimum; class B is differentiable but should not be solved by gradients; for class C, giving up gradients in favor of statistical certification is **the consensus practice of the literature**, not a compromise.

2. **The most critical finding is that the two sets barely intersect.** Where RL shows task-agnostic vitality (§6.4.1: calibration, confidence, constraint satisfaction) **is exactly classes A/C**, where a differentiable proxy or post-hoc certification already exists; and the truly non-differentiable class D **is exactly where RL's measured gain is zero** (GRPO no gain, the controller only adds cost). Therefore the chain of reasoning "because our objective is non-differentiable, we should use RL" **does not hold under the existing evidence**: non-differentiability and RL's gain surface do not line up.

3. **Class B offers a cheaper path.** Consistency-type expectations (order, IIA, definition priority) are better handled by construction than by writing pairwise losses and training: the effect sizes of Prism-1 and block-causal are **orders of magnitude** (8.30%→0.11%, zero flips), while the effect size of loss terms is "accuracy unchanged, robustness improved". The conclusion of §6.1.3, "structural changes are the only algorithmic changes with a large effect size", holds here once again.

#### 6.4.2.2 Under what conditions this answer would change

- If **sampled intermediate objects** (reasoning chains, example-set edits, tool calls) are introduced before the readout, class D's credit assignment gains a landing point and the applicability condition for RL is met (§6.3.4: OpenJev-RLCD's "calibrate first, then reinforce", StartLux AutoThink, Jev-LDE).
- If **business utility** is written directly into the objective (rather than proxied by a verifier), class A can expand: Releasing the Inner Jev tried an expected-utility objective, but got only **+0.9±0.9 pp** ([2610.17205](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17205)), i.e. indistinguishable from zero.
- If the six definition disagreements of §1.4 are fixed, part of class E becomes measurable quantities and may thereby enter class C (certifiable).

### 6.4.3 Blind spots identified in this section

1. **Nobody has run a controlled comparison of "reward = real objective" against "reward = verifier proxy" on a single-pass decision model**; the arbitrage evidence of 16.1.3 all comes from generative instruction following.
2. **Class D credit assignment has no methodological work at all on the Jev-style interface**: once the decision model is placed in an Agent loop, the only thing tried has been GRPO, with no gain.
3. **Class B's "structure vs loss" has no same-backbone comparison**: block-causal and Prism-1 were each compared against "training with shuffled order", but neither has been compared against a PACT-style pairwise loss on the same backbone and the same data.
4. **Class C's certification methods are untested on hybrid backbones**: SHIFT-JEV uses Qwen3-0.6B/4B and Phi-4-mini, all attention-only.
5. **The boundary "second-order properties generalize, first-order knowledge does not" has itself not been tested head-on by any paper**; it is induced by this section from five independent pieces of evidence and is a hypothesis awaiting verification, not an established conclusion.
## 6.5 Training objective: what the output should look like, and whether to introduce chain of thought

> Protocol: still analysis and survey only. This section answers the second follow-up question: **what the training objective of a Jev-class model is, what numeric / decision outputs should look like, how to optimize them, whether chain of thought should be introduced to improve decision quality; and, if the business goal is System 1, what that chain of thought should look like**. The full landscape of learning algorithms is already in §6.1 (seven classes of change A–G and the ablation evidence); this section does not repeat it and only answers the two things §6.1 did not expand on: "the shape of the output" and "chain of thought".
>
> Conclusions first: (1) **the consensus on the training objective has converged on "proper scoring rules", but what is really missing is not the objective function but the shape of the output space**: three of the four output kinds have the wrong shape (boolean lacks the third truth value; the Choice readout is ordinal but is used as a probability; Score is systematically compressed), and numeric values should not be fit with a single scalar at all; (2) **chain of thought should be introduced, but not at inference time**: in the existing evidence the three uses of chain of thought (distillation at training time, generation at inference time, layer recurrence) have diminishing returns and rising cost, and the most underrated is a fourth one, **letting Jev read others' chains**; (3) if the business goal is System 1, then its "chain of thought" **at inference time should not be a token stream but a question graph**: on a prefix-only model asking one more question is nearly free, while generating one more token is metered.
>
> **Re-check note (§8.1)**: the "question graph vs token stream" of this section is the report's own construct, derived from the cost structure rather than measured (§8.1.2, overfitting risk medium-high); the compositional calibration of "splitting one judgment into n judgments" has not been answered by any paper. The most stable conclusions of this section are ordinal compression and "structural constraints beat loss terms"; the one most likely to be overturned is the ranking of the three numeric routes (§8.1.4).

### 6.5.1 The consensus objective and its three proven boundaries

§6.1.2 already gave the conclusion: relative to ordinary classification fine-tuning, the changes concentrate on the **readout position** and on **adding a proper-scoring-rule term to the objective**. This section only adds three "already proven" boundaries, because they directly constrain all the discussion in §6.5.2–18.5.

1. **When labels are known, the proper scoring rule and the policy gradient of an RLCD-style reward reach the same optimum, without the variance of the latter** (NDM §1.3.1, [2610.25553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25553)). → On a pure readout, "switching to RL" is not an objective-function question but a variance question (§6.3.4, §6.4.1.3).
2. **When the uncertainty comes from annotator disagreement, the same objective provably cannot beat cross-entropy** ([2609.38850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38850)). → Wherever the business labels themselves are disputed (moderation, scoring, satisfaction), there is no room to gain on the objective-function side.
3. **Adding a proper-scoring-rule term does nothing for accuracy and helps robustness and NLL**: in SBERT2S1's two-head × five-objective grid, after temperature scaling no objective is clearly better calibrated than cross-entropy ([2610.02486](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02486)); none of PACT's four terms raises raw accuracy, the gains are seed variance halved and NLL −26% ([2609.35865](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35865)); the expected-utility objective gives +0.9±0.9 pp ([2610.17205](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17205)).

**Corollary**: if one wants "decision quality" to actually improve, the three movable places are, in order, **the shape of the output space** (§6.5.2), **structural constraints** (§6.1.3 already proved them the most effective), and **the form of computation at inference time** (§6.5.4). The objective function itself has essentially topped out.

### 6.5.2 What each of the four outputs "should look like"

#### 6.5.2.1 Boolean / Noul: the shape is wrong because a truth value is missing

Sys1Cal-v1 takes true/false propositions whose P(A) is "known by construction", asks each of them through the three primitives, scores by total variation distance to the true distribution, and finds a behavior that can only be explained by "Jev suppresses the third truth value": in `Choice` answers P(A) and P(¬A) are presented as summing to 1, while the sum is missing a term P(U)≠0; **restoring P(U) raises the median soft accuracy of Choice from 0.771 to 0.978** ([2609.35342](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35342)).

The same conclusion is independently verified from the "probability axioms" side: on 480 negation pairs the sum of "the label is X" and "the label is not X" deviates from 1 by 0.064 on average (95% CI 0.055–0.072), the three single-label probabilities sum to **1.14** on average, and the violation is about 5 times its repeat noise and **concentrated where it is uncertain** ([2609.33209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33209)). By comparison, the first-token readout of the official BF16 weights of Qwen3.8-27B deviates by 0.293 and its verbalized probability by 0.122, and it is a **two-sided negator** (in 196 of the 480 pairs it negates both a proposition and its negation), and it is not more self-consistent where it is more confident.

**Shape conclusion**: the correct shape of a boolean output is not P(true) but **a distribution over the triple {true, false, unknown}**; and the "uncertain" term cannot be cut out afterwards by a threshold, because the violation is concentrated precisely in the uncertain region. D4's "refusal / fallback" sub-dimension (§4.2) and P6/P7 (§2.1) converge here: [2610.19856](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19856) measures that "correctly recognizing the category does not transfer into correctly selecting the fallback option", precisely because the fallback is implemented as **an ordinary option in the option set** rather than **a structural slot in the distribution**.

#### 6.5.2.2 Selection / Choice: the readout is ordinal, and using it as a probability goes wrong

JevCascade proves a testable property: when the intervention changes only the menu and the text is unchanged, **post-intervention accuracy is already determined by the cached first-pass distribution**: restrict the first-pass probabilities to the new menu, renormalize, and take the argmax, with no labels and no second pass; across six model families, ten core datasets (sixteen in total) and two tasks, menu-only interventions are predicted to within **4.2 points**, exactly for one family. The key qualification is: **this property is ordinal, not probabilistic**: the "probability-layer" variant of the same estimator has an error of **21.0 points**; and it is category-specific, since generative models of the same size have errors of 1.6–15.8 points that get worse with scale ([2610.07716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07716)).

Two direct consequences:

- **Menu design is a stronger lever than model size**: 0.8B on a curated 5-candidate menu reaches 95.4% on CLINC150, 4B on the full 150-label menu 80.0% (same paper). This agrees with the NIOCIS ablation: the gain is attributed to **restricting the candidate set per node** (+3.5 points over a flat catalog) rather than to graph traversal (zero within the interval) ([2610.25985](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25985)).
- **Using Choice probabilities as "probabilities" is out-of-scope use**. TypedBench measured the business consequence: under asymmetric costs, **using the host model's probabilities can be worse than taking its top-1 directly** ([2610.11392](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11392)).

**Shape conclusion**: the Choice output should be treated as **a ranking over the given menu + a confidence valid only on that menu**, not as a probability statement about the world. The property "probabilities remain comparable after the menu changes" does not hold under the existing evidence (IIA violation, §4.2).

#### 6.5.2.3 Score: ordinal compression is systematic, and scale does not fix it

This is the only output-shape defect in the existing literature that has been **confirmed on forty datasets across three model families**.

| Observation | Number | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinal scale-utilization bias | 40 datasets, nine models from the three families JEV / KEV / StartLux; final decisions concentrate on **fewer effective labels** than the gold distribution; clearly weaker on unordered semantic categories | [2609.38827](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38827) |
| A finer scale **worsens** compression | The same items are compressed more strongly on a finer ordinal scale, with gold imbalance and candidate position controlled | Same |
| Randomizing candidate order | **Reduces but does not eliminate** compression | Same |
| Widening ordinal spacing at a fixed number of candidates | Can raise accuracy **without restoring utilization** | Same |
| Scale | Larger models have better accuracy but **do not consistently restore scale utilization** | Same |
| Co-occurring phenomenon | For most models, **concentrated decisions co-occur with wide average candidate probabilities** | Same |
| Can be mitigated by targeted training | The authors conclude that "scale utilization should stand alongside accuracy as an actionable reliability target" | Same |
| Not Jev-specific | Three frontier multimodal LLM families show the same **central-tendency effect** (endpoint compression) on clinical ordinal scales: overestimating the low end and underestimating the high end, while a fully fine-tuned ViT is best calibrated (MAE 0.52, 91% within tolerance); few-shot examples spanning the full range and removing clinical terminology **both fail to eliminate** the effect | [2605.16386](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16386) |
| A direct objective-function lever | PACT's **ordinal transport cost** term lowers the MAE of rubric fields to **0.232** (vs 0.311 for the published recipe), with the lowest position bias (9.8% vs 13.8% changed verdicts under relabeling) and flat accuracy (84.6% vs 85.2%, McNemar p ≥ 0.50 for every seed) | [2609.35865](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35865) |
| Almost nobody turns it on in public implementations | Kev's `--ord_w` (ordinal loss) is **unused in the released models** (§6.1.1 table, row B) | [jaredpalmer/kev](https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev) |
| The readout form itself moves the result | Asking the same question with `Score` instead of `Choice` moves grading accuracy by up to **10.5 points** | [2609.32160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32160), paraphrasing T3 |
| Do not collapse the distribution into one number | Psychometric audit: **under the default rounded readout, 13 of 14 decision models are below the generative reference**; yet after averaging over respondents, **the probability vector is closer to the human item marginals than the rounded answers**, and in ranking respondents it is roughly equal to a profile regression fit on 163 real people | [2610.14912](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14912) |

**Shape conclusion**: the correct shape of Score is **a full distribution over ordered levels**; training should carry an ordinal term (transport cost / ordinal cross-entropy), evaluation should use an **ordinal proper scoring rule** rather than accuracy (TypedBench does exactly this, [2610.11392](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11392)), and downstream **should not round to a single level**. These three are the best-evidenced group in this section and the worst-implemented in public code.

#### 6.5.2.4 Numeric: Jev is not a regressor; there are three viable routes and one forbidden zone

First, the forbidden zone.

- **The arithmetic refusal bottleneck**: accuracy is 99% when the answer is present, but the correct refusal rate when the answer is absent is only **7%**, and this gap persists across numeric magnitude, operation depth, context phrasing and refusal label, and extends to time computation and capacity rounding; yet **on the same answer-absent arithmetic problems, native boolean verification reaches 99% exact match**, which means **classification-style refusal fails even though the model can successfully verify the correctness of a candidate**; a decision threshold selected on independent development problems raises refusal accuracy from 7% to **79%** while keeping 97% answer-present accuracy, with no retraining or extra inference ([2609.39496](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39496)).
- **The readout vs reasoning gap is largest on dates and numbers**: 56–66% (readout) vs 98–100% (reasoning) (§6.2 L3).
- **In medicine this is an explicit capability boundary**: Jev is on par with leading models on text-driven tasks but clearly weaker on **numerically driven** tasks ([2610.18529](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18529)).
- §1.1.6 already wrote this as a conclusion: Jev **has no arithmetic / date / counting capability**.

Outside the "forbidden zone", the literature offers three genuinely viable numeric output shapes.

| Route | Method | Measured quantity | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **R1 Turn the number into an interval decision (training-free)** | Accept that "selecting a numeric interval is itself a decision problem"; use a **multi-way decision tree to recursively refine the interval**, always keeping the original question in context, with no parameter updates and no access to hidden states; on a 100-value grid a ten-way tree needs only two rounds | Interval-normalized MAE **1.84%** vs 5.18% for direct selection; **2.93 pp higher even than "direct selection with the correct answer in the candidate table"**; in a three-date historical-index study the mean relative recall error is 4.58%, and the readout error when the value is given is **0%** | [2609.28587](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28587) |
| **R2 Turn the number into binned classification, coarse to fine** | For numeric and point-prediction tasks use **value bins and image regions**, refined by coarse-to-fine classification; multiple-choice tasks use the given options directly | This readout form is the core mechanism of MJev-Spatial | [2610.22276](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22276) |
| **R3 Use a decoding-style regression head** | Represent the numeric prediction as a decoded string; give the theoretical grounds for this capability and treat a causal sequence-decoding model as a numeric regression head over arbitrary feature representations | Although trained only with ordinary next-token cross-entropy, **the decoding head is as good as standard pointwise heads on standard regression tasks**, while being flexible enough to capture smooth numeric distributions (e.g. density estimation) | [2501.19383](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.19383) |

**Shape conclusion**: numeric output should be **a distribution over ordered bins** (the same family as Score); recursive refinement can buy precision, and a decoding head can buy smooth densities; but it **should not be a scalar regression target** and **should not carry arithmetic**. "Whether to support numbers" is therefore not a model-capability question but an **interface decomposition question**: hand "computing" to tools or System 2, and hand "which interval it falls in" to the readout.

#### 6.5.2.5 Summary: four criteria for output shape

| Primitive | Current shape | Evidence-supported shape | Strongest counterexample |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Noul | P(true) ∈ [0,1] | Distribution over {true, false, **unknown**} | Soft accuracy 0.771 → 0.978 after restoring P(U) ([2609.35342](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35342)) |
| Choice | Probability distribution over options | **Ranking over the given menu** + within-menu confidence | Probability-layer estimation error 21.0 points vs ordinal-layer 4.2 points ([2610.07716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07716)); under asymmetric costs probabilities are worse than top-1 ([2610.11392](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11392)) |
| Score | Distribution over ordered levels (but compressed, and often rounded downstream) | Distribution over ordered levels + **ordinal term** + **not collapsed into one number** | Scale-utilization bias on 40 datasets and 9 models ([2609.38827](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38827)); probability vectors closer to human marginals than rounding ([2610.14912](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14912)) |
| Numeric | Not in the interface | **Distribution over ordered bins** (recursive refinement / decoding head), arithmetic outsourced | Refusal rate 7% ([2609.39496](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39496)); interval decision MAE 1.84% vs 5.18% ([2609.28587](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28587)) |

### 6.5.3 How to optimize: what the evidence supports and what it rules out

| Lever | Evidence | Judgment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Structural invariance by construction** (block-causal attention, position reset, one independent block per option) | Flip rate 8.30% → 0.11% (Gemma 3 1B), 18.16% → 1.20% (Qwen3 1.7B); Prism-1 has **zero flips by construction** and the Luce axiom holds; NDM's block attention mask makes each answer invariant to option order **by construction** and independent of the other questions, with lower loss than a pairwise cross-encoder at equal compute (§6.1.3; [2610.01601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01601), [2610.22066](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22066), [2610.25553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25553)) | **Most effective**. ### 6.5.4 Whether to introduce chain of thought: four questions conflated into one
"Introducing chain of thought" is four different things in the literature, with different gains and different costs.

#### 6.5.4.1 Use A: as a supervision source at training time, absent at inference time (strongest evidence)

**Reasoning-to-Readout self-distillation**: internalize an open-weight LLM's **own reasoning** into a single-pass first-token decision. SII-Decision-27B is **on par with Jev** on JEVal, while a typical query takes about **0.1 s** ([2610.03935](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03935)).

Evidence from the same family: Eikos adds a **reasoning-rationale auxiliary loss** on top of soft targets (§6.1.1 table, row C, [2610.29052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29052)); in StartLux's three-stage pipeline, AutoThink's reasoning ability comes from **reasoning supervision + RL** ([2610.17309](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17309)); FunDM's label-free KL distillation recovers 99% of the tuning gain ([2610.17175](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17175)).

**Judgment**: this is the only use that "gets the reasoning gain without paying the reasoning cost", and the only one that does not conflict with the business goal (the unit price of §2.1).

#### 6.5.4.2 Use B: generate chain of thought at inference time (effective, but then it is no longer System 1)

| Evidence | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Off-the-shelf models suffice | LLM-as-Jev points out that decision models "seamlessly inherit the backbone's thinking mode": Jev is introduced as a System 1 model, while "Thinking Jev" uses **budgeted test-time reasoning** to raise accuracy on hard tasks, **with no training** ([2610.02076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02076)) |
| A purpose-trained version | StartLux AutoThink invokes explicit reasoning on uncertain decisions and answers directly in confident cases; on DecisionPivot it is **11.5–18.4 points** of paired accuracy above the corresponding Instant version and leads among evaluated systems; the 27B AutoThink reaches 73.4 on the Decision Index 0.3 public subset ([2610.17309](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17309)) |
| The cost must be explicitly held down | RLCD² uses a frozen model and a proper-scoring-rule reward for "improvement in decision probability mass", and applies a **soft penalty** to overlong reasoning traces: the share of traces terminating within the length limit rises by up to **+48 pp**, mean reasoning length falls by up to **−65%**, and decision accuracy is essentially preserved (same paper) |
| Calibration cost | With an RLVR-style outcome reward, there is extreme overconfidence and the decision token carries no confidence information ([2601.13284](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13284)); RLCD without prior calibration either System-One collapses or is swamped by the policy gradient ([2609.38850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38850)) |

**Judgment**: effective, but it moves the cost structure back to the generative side (§5.1.1.1 eliminates exactly the decoding cost), so it can only exist as **a confidence-gated minority path**: this is cell A of §5.1.4, and the two properties P3/P5 that cell A depends on cannot be directly promised (§5.1.2).

#### 6.5.4.3 Use C: layer recurrence, i.e. "chain of thought without generating tokens" (System 1.5)

| Evidence | Content |
| --- | --- |
| SanSi | Turns a pretrained recurrent language model into a typed decision model; **option probabilities are read after every recurrence, and every recurrence is trained with a proper scoring rule**, so one training run yields a model covering every budget from 1 to 8 recurrences. **72.0%** on 10,027 test decisions from 59 sources: **13.5 points** above a non-recurrent model of the same shape (same recipe), 5.3 points above a newer non-recurrent model of the same size, 1.8 points below a model with three times the parameters. On two depth-controlled tasks, **recurrence pushes the solvable depth beyond the depths seen in training**, while larger single-pass models fail. As a judge for RL policy optimization (no gold answers), it raises the generator's share of correct answers from 37% to 52% ([2610.07730](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07730)) |
| LEV | Same idea, three recurrent backbones: on the same set of weights accuracy rises with depth, and **the gain falls mainly on hard items**; 2.6B goes from 63.2% at depth 1 to **76.2%** at depth 4 (JevBench). **On-demand depth** (decided per item by a small stopper with a threshold fit on unlabeled requests) saves **15%–24%** compute at a cost of under one accuracy point, on par with or slightly above a fixed-depth mixture; on the locked test set, 1.4B and 3B are **5.4–8.8 points** above non-recurrent dense models and cascades at **equal latency (<50 ms)** ([2610.20951](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20951)) |

**Judgment**: this is **one of the most direct validated answers** to "if the business goal is System 1, what should the chain of thought look like": variable depth, zero output tokens, controllable latency, allocated per item. Its constraint is the backbone: both are built on **language models pretrained to be recurrent** (Ouro), while our base model family (Qwen3.5 / 3.8) is not recurrently pretrained and is a Gated DeltaNet hybrid (§1.3). There is no work in the literature on "converting a non-recurrent hybrid backbone into a recurrent readout"; this is a zero hit.

#### 6.5.4.4 Use D: let Jev read **others'** chains of thought (most underrated)

On seven LLM reasoners × five multiple-choice benchmarks (35 pairs), Jev is used as a "decision reader for truncated reasoning": across all tested budgets from 500 to 16,000 tokens, **Jev's mean accuracy is above forced answering** (with the estimated cost of reasoning and answering counted in). At a 1,500-token budget, **+4.1 pp** overall and **+8.6 pp** on truncated items, 17 pairs significant under Holm-corrected bootstrap; offline replay estimates 34.5% of the full-run cost with only 1.13 pp of accuracy lost; a policy selected on the training fold saves **71.3%** of the estimated full-run cost on the held-out fold while losing only 0.58 pp. The same paper also finds that Jev's **question-only** accuracy is strong, on average above 500-token forced answering (68.0% vs 65.5%), but this advantage varies by benchmark ([2610.25768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25768)).

**Judgment**: this is the form that fully decouples "chain of thought" from "decision": **the chain is produced by System 2 and the decision is consumed by System 1**. It does not require Jev to reason itself, only to read; and among the property checklist of §5.1.2 it depends only on P1 and P3.

#### 6.5.4.5 Why some form of "deeper computation" must be introduced: the hard limit

Jev **cannot make the decisions that "an LLM can only make by writing out intermediate steps"**. On a factor-by-factor benchmark, Jev finds and selects the record without error within 20k tokens and 64 options, but once the answer depends on **more than two updates** from the same user, accuracy drops, reaching 53%–80% at sixteen updates, because similar records interfere with one another. Given a **stale state summary**, it answers according to the stale summary. On real decision items graded by "how many thinking tokens a reference LLM needs", Jev gets 97.5% of the items that the LLM **can solve in one forward pass**, but only **74%** of the rest. More critically, **it does not know its own limit**: its confidence can flag its own errors, but cannot flag the errors it inherits from stale inputs, and neither explicit nor trained handoff options **catch the latter**. All evaluated open models share these limits, and the authors conclude that "**checking the state such models depend on requires a model that reasons**" ([2610.24929](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24929)).

**This limit decides the trade-off among the uses**: A (training-time distillation) cannot break through it, because what is distilled is the "solvable in one forward pass" part; C (layer recurrence) is the only use with **depth-extrapolation evidence** (SanSi pushes the solvable depth beyond the training depth); B and D are two ways of outsourcing the limit to System 2.

### 6.5.5 If the business goal is System 1, what should this chain of thought look like

Lay out the four uses of §6.5.4 by "whether decoding cost is paid" and "whether the one-forward-pass limit is broken", and the answer appears.

| Use | Decoding cost at inference | Can it break the one-forward-pass limit | Suited for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A Training-time distillation | **No** | No | Default plan: gets the full gain of the "solvable in one forward pass" part for free |
| C Layer recurrence (System 1.5) | **No** (pays layer recomputation) | **Partly** (depth extrapolation validated) | The only validated route to more depth under the zero-token premise |
| D Reading others' chains | No (S2 pays for the chain) | Yes (the limit is carried by S2) | Pipelines that already have an S2 running |
| B Generating its own chain | **Yes** | Yes | Only as a confidence-gated minority path |

But there is a **fifth form**, and it is the natural form of "the chain of thought of System 1" at the interface level.

#### 6.5.5.1 The chain of thought of System 1 is a question graph, not a token stream

The cost structure of a prefix-only model decides this: **all questions in one request are answered in the same forward pass** (§1.1.4), so asking one more question is nearly free (in Jev's order; the order fixed in §2.2.1 trades this for cross-sample caching along the rule axis, so each node of the question graph pays the prefill of its candidate branches once); while generating one more token is metered (§5.1.1.1). So the equivalent of "unrolling the reasoning" on Jev is not "writing out the steps" but **decomposing one judgment into more typed judgments and putting their structure into the request or into the caller's code**.

Existing instances already cover most variants of this form:

| Form | Method | Measured quantity | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Parallel sub-questions, read the disagreement** | Because "the model's price for extra questions is almost zero", decompose the judgment into parallel sub-questions and read their dispersion; this recovers the **human disagreement** signal that the model's native confidence misses | The remedy **is specific**: it helps only the weakest, disagreement-blind models, and only when the decomposition aligns with the source of disagreement; dedicated toxicity models and the strongest LLMs do not need it, and the effect is distribution-sensitive | [2610.33258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33258) |
| **Put "what to ask" into the same forward pass too** | LAVOIR puts candidate missing information (slots) in the input, right next to the answer options, so that **one forward pass returns both the decision distribution and, for each slot, the "expected gain in correct-decision probability if that slot were asked about"**; the VOI target needs no human labels; a Gini-impurity upper bound constrains predictions to the range "a calibrated model can still gain" | The questioning policy matches a greedy oracle VOI policy (AUC 0.799 vs 0.797); at most 0.5 follow-ups per dialogue, it is **14.1 points** above "never ask"; on real ABCD dialogues, one real interaction raises accuracy by **8.3 points** where it chooses to ask and leaves it unchanged where it does not; on SGD the bound cuts the follow-up rate from 93% to **8.6%**; 31 ms (median, GH200) | [2609.30706](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30706) |
| **Walk the tree node by node** | SemTree's tree of Yes/No/Unknown nodes can localize and repair failure conditions node by node; Jev-Guard first routes on an editable policy tree and then does conditional classification; the official docs' "walk the classification tree level by level" | Jev-Guard: safety accuracy 93.72% on 7,033 cases, mean latency 58 ms, hierarchical routing accuracy 93.10%, 240 ms end to end | [2610.13999](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13999), [2610.18326](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18326) |
| **Menu curation** | Shrink the candidate set before asking; this is a stronger lever than deepening the model | 0.8B with a curated 5-candidate menu 95.4% vs 4B with all 150 labels 80.0%; restricting the candidate set per node +3.5 points | [2610.07716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07716), [2610.25985](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25985) |
| **Reuse the first-pass distribution** | Menu-only interventions **need no second forward pass**; restricting + renormalizing the first-pass distribution suffices, within 4.2 points of error | The same paper also gives the negative side: **running uniformly more passes buys only calibration, almost no accuracy**; at equal cost a confidence cascade beats any scheme that re-asks the same model | [2610.07716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07716) |

#### 6.5.5.2 Three known boundaries of this form

1. **Decomposition is not a universal gain.** "Running uniformly more passes buys only calibration, almost no accuracy" ([2610.07716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07716)); reading disagreement from parallel sub-questions works only under specific conditions ([2610.33258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33258)).
2. **Decomposition amplifies consistency defects.** The question form itself moves the answer (`Score` vs `Choice` by up to 10.5 points, [2609.32160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32160)), and the probabilities are already not self-consistent (three single-label probabilities summing to 1.14, [2609.33209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33209)). Splitting one judgment into ten and recombining them multiplies these biases ten times. The "decomposition consistency" sub-dimension of §4.2 §D4 exists for exactly this.
3. **Correct intermediate choices do not guarantee a correct sequence**: a correct final label masks wrong intermediate choices ([2609.32160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32160), paraphrasing T2); Choice2Seq measures "task correctness, plan faithfulness, output compliance" separately ([2610.16191](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16191)).

**§6.5.5 conclusion**: if the business goal is System 1, then the "chain of thought" should be absorbed **at training time** in the form of distillation (use A), and should exist **at inference time** in two zero-token forms: **the depth of layer recurrence** (use C) and **the width of the question graph** (§6.5.5.1); a chain of thought that actually generates text should only be a confidence-gated escape route (use B), or simply be produced by System 2 with Jev only responsible for reading it (use D).

### 6.5.6 Section summary and blind spots

**Training objective**: proper scoring rules are the consensus and have topped out (three boundaries in §6.5.1). What can be moved is the shape of the output space, structural constraints, and the form of computation at inference time.

**Output shape**: boolean needs to be ternary (with unknown); Choice is an ordinal quantity within a menu, not a world probability; Score needs an ordinal term and must not be rounded downstream; numbers must land on ordered bins with arithmetic outsourced. Three of the four have evidence spanning many datasets and many models, and are the worst-implemented in public code (Kev's ordinal loss was not enabled at release).

**Chain of thought**: introduce it, but at inference time it should not be a token stream. Of the four uses, A (training-time distillation) is the default, C (layer recurrence) is the only zero-token route with depth-extrapolation evidence, D (reading others' chains) is the most underrated, and B (generating its own) only deserves to be a gated escape route. And System 1's own form of "unrolling" is the **question graph**: asking more is nearly free, generating more is metered.

**Blind spots**:

1. **Nobody has made "unknown" a structural slot in the interface and measured it**. P(U) has only been **restored after the fact** once ([2609.35342](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35342)); no training work has taken the ternary truth value as the output space.
2. **The gain from the ordinal term has evidence from only one paper and one setting**. PACT's MAE 0.232 vs 0.311 comes from a frozen held-out set of 324 items and three seeds ([2609.35865](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35865)); [2609.38827](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38827) says it "can be mitigated by targeted training" but gives no cross-dataset training comparison. **The number "how much does scale-utilization bias drop once the ordinal loss is turned on" does not exist.**
3. **The three numeric routes are not compared against each other**. R1 is training-free, R2 is in multimodal, R3 is in general regression; **no paper has compared the interval decision tree, binned classification and the decoding regression head on the same batch of numeric decisions**.
4. **Layer recurrence is incompatible with our backbone, and nobody has studied the bridge**. SanSi / LEV both require a recurrently pretrained backbone; "converting a non-recurrent hybrid (Gated DeltaNet + attention) backbone into a multi-recurrence readout" is a zero hit (§1.3).
5. **The compositional semantics of the question graph has no theory**. Decomposition multiplies consistency defects (§6.5.5.2), and existing work has only measured single-level decomposition; "what is the calibration of n typed judgments after composition" has been answered by no paper; the 160 items and 480 pairs of [2609.33209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33209) are the only evidence in the whole literature on probabilistic self-consistency.
6. **The mechanism "the decision token carries no confidence information" has only been diagnosed in the generative RLVR setting** ([2601.13284](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13284)). Whether it also holds on a pure-readout (no reasoning chain) single-pass model is unverified; and if it does hold, it directly explains the "RL negative or ineffective on single-pass models" recorded in §6.1.3.
